# Exemplar Exam Essay 1: The Political Economy of Information in Malawi (1964–1994)

**Question**: *Critically discuss the political economy of information with reference to Paul Sturges’ analysis of Malawi between 1964 and 1994. In your answer, examine how state control of information serves as an instrument of political power, and evaluate the resulting consequences for society. [20 Marks]*

---

## Model Answer (Full 20/20 Mark Rubric)

### 1. Introduction and Theoretical Grounding
In classical political economy, information is often treated as an intangible commodity or an enabler of democratic decision-making. However, as demonstrated by Paul Sturges (1998) in his seminal analysis *The Political Economy of Information: Malawi 1964–1994*, information within authoritarian regimes is primarily an instrument of state hegemony and political survival. Rather than facilitating free civic discourse, the state treats information as a strategic monopoly where access is strictly engineered to entrench power, suppress political pluralism, and engineer societal compliance.

### 2. State Control Mechanisms Under Dr. Hastings Kamuzu Banda
Following Malawi’s independence in 1964, President Dr. Hastings Kamuzu Banda established a 30-year totalitarian regime characterized by comprehensive information enclosure. Sturges outlines three primary mechanisms through which this political economy of control operated:

1. **The Censorship Board as an Epistemic Gatekeeper**: The Malawi Censorship Board exercised unconstrained discretionary power to ban foreign and domestic literature, newspapers, theatrical productions, and musical recordings. Publications referencing civil unrest, democratic dissent, or alternative socio-political ideologies were systematically criminalized.
2. **The Monopoly of Communication Channels**: The state nationalized broadcasting through the Malawi Broadcasting Corporation (MBC), turning radio into an exclusive propaganda organ. Television was banned entirely until the late 1990s, while telephone networks and private postal correspondence were actively monitored by state security intelligence.
3. **Criminalization of Possession and Self-Censorship**: Possession of proscribed texts resulted in detention without trial, political exile, or execution. This created an all-pervasive culture of fear and self-censorship among journalists, academics, civil servants, and librarians.

### 3. Societal and Economic Consequences: The Creation of "Information Poverty"
The deliberate suppression of information had catastrophic structural consequences for Malawian society:
* **Severe Information Poverty**: Citizens were deprived of critical medical, agricultural, and socio-economic intelligence necessary for human development. Independent scientific research was suppressed if its findings contradicted state narratives.
* **Erosion of Academic Freedom**: Universities were stripped of autonomy; curricula were vetted to ensure loyalty to the ruling Malawi Congress Party (MCP), severely impairing human capital development.
* **Suppression of Civil Society**: Without horizontal communication channels, trade unions, civic associations, and opposition parties could not organize, sustaining Banda's dictatorship for three decades until external economic pressures and domestic resistance forced multi-party elections in 1994.

### 4. Critical Synthesis and Modern Democratic Lessons
Sturges’ analysis reveals that a society’s "information environment" is never neutral—it directly reflects its underlying political economy. In modern sub-Saharan Africa, while state-enforced physical censorship has largely been dismantled, the political economy of information faces contemporary mutations. Today, state power manifests through internet shutdowns during elections, excessive social media taxation, and cyber-surveillance. The lesson of Malawi 1964–1994 remains fundamental: democratic governance requires not merely access to technology, but the structural, legal, and constitutional protection of open, uncensored information flows.
