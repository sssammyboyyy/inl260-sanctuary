# Theme 3: Microeconomics: Information as an Economic Good

## 🎯 Syllabus Outcomes (Study Guide LO 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
* **Required Reading**: Shapiro & Varian *Information Rules*; Camm et al.
* **Exam Section**: Short Questions (Themes 1, 3, 4, 6) & MCQs.

## 📖 The Narrative Hook
Imagine baking a loaf of bread vs developing Spotify. To make a second loaf of bread, you need more flour, yeast, electricity, and an oven. But once Spotify writes its audio streaming algorithm and buys its servers, the cost of sending that song to customer #2 or customer #20,000,000 is **literally R0.00**. How do you price and sell something that costs nothing to reproduce?

---

## 🏛️ Core Academic Frameworks

### 1. The Cost Structure of Information Goods
* **High Fixed Sunk Costs ($FC$)**: Producing the *first copy* of an information good requires immense capital, research, programming time, or creative effort. These costs are "sunk"—they cannot be recovered if the product flops.
  * *Example*: Spending $200 million to code Grand Theft Auto or research a pharmaceutical patent.
* **Near-Zero Marginal Costs ($MC \approx 0$)**: The cost of reproducing and distributing the second, third, or millionth copy of digital information is virtually zero.
  * *Consequence*: Traditional competitive pricing where $\text{Price} = \text{Marginal Cost}$ would mean charging R0.00! Firms must use pricing strategies that ignore marginal costs.

### 2. Economic Properties of Information
1. **Non-Rivalrous**: Unlike an apple (if I eat it, you cannot), one person consuming an e-book or stream does not diminish or deplete its availability to anyone else.
2. **Experience Good**: A consumer cannot determine the quality, truth, or utility of information until after they have consumed/read it. (Hence the necessity of reviews, free previews, and trial periods).
3. **Excludability via Technology/Law**: Information is naturally non-excludable (easy to copy/leak), so firms artificially enforce excludability via copyright laws, encryption, passwords, and paywalls.
4. **Network Effects (Metcalfe's Law)**: The value of an information network grows exponentially with the square of the number of users ($V \propto n^2$). (e.g. WhatsApp is worthless if only one person uses it; it becomes indispensable when everyone is on it).

### 3. Pricing Strategies for Information Goods
When marginal cost is zero, how do companies maximize profit?
* **Versioning (Price Discrimination)**: Offering different tiers tailored to different customer valuations:
  * Free / Basic Edition vs Professional vs Enterprise.
  * Academic license vs Corporate license.
  * Adding friction or delay to lower tiers (e.g. ads on free Spotify; ad-free on Premium).
* **Product Bundling**: Combining multiple distinct information goods into a single package at a fixed subscription price:
  * *Example*: Microsoft 365 (Word + Excel + PowerPoint + Cloud storage) or Netflix catalog. Bundling reduces customer valuation variance and increases total revenue.
* **Lock-in and High Switching Costs**: Making it painful or expensive for users to switch to a competitor:
  * Proprietary file formats, learning curves, accumulated personal data history (e.g. your 10-year Spotify playlist history).

### 4. Infonomics vs. Neoclassical Economic Theory
* **Neoclassical Theory**: Treats goods as physical commodities that suffer from physical wear-and-tear, depreciation, and diminishing returns.
* **Infonomics (Douglas Laney)**: The discipline of treating information as an actual quantifiable corporate balance-sheet asset. Information does **not** wear out; instead, it can be regenerated, shared, and combined infinitely without physical depletion.

## ⚡ Examiner Trap Alert
* ⚠️ **Trap**: If asked "Why can't information goods use cost-plus pricing?", the answer is: *Because marginal cost is approximately zero ($MC \approx 0$). If price equaled marginal cost, revenue would be zero and fixed creation costs could never be recovered!*
